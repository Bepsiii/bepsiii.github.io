VZMORE iX9 Max

The r/PcBuild Review

Hey Gang, im once again back with a review, this time on the sister to the AX9 Max, the iX9 Max!

Disclosure: VZMORE supplied this review unit free of charge. No monetary payment was received for this review. The testing, findings and verdict are my own.

Originally posted in r/PcBuild. Read the original review and discussion on Reddit ↗

1. Introduction

Following my AX9 Max review, I tested the VZMORE iX9 Max to see how its Intel Core Ultra 9 185H handled everyday use, sustained loads, gaming and local AI. I was particularly interested in its cooling, connectivity and how far the fixed 24GB of RAM would stretch.

Price at the time of the review: US MSRP $949 for the 24GB RAM / 1TB SSD configuration.

Who is VZMORE?

VZMORE is a newer contender in the mini-PC space whose AX9 Max and iX9 Max focus on compact systems with vapour chamber cooling. The iX9 Max is aimed at productivity, creative work and development. Its dual Ethernet ports and storage expansion also make it interesting for a home lab, although the fixed 24GB of RAM is an important limit for larger virtual machines and local AI workloads.

2. Unboxing and Industrial Design

Packaging and Included Accessories

The iX9 Max ships in a minimalist matte black presentation box sporting the outline of the device and the iX9 Max identifier, secured with tamper seals. Opening the magnetic lid reveals the main unit seated firmly inside a custom card insert, with all accessories neatly arranged in separate compartments and wrapped in paper.

Alongside the PC, VZMORE includes a practical set of accessories:

  • 1x 120W compact DC barrel-jack power adapter
  • 1x HDMI cable (1.0m)
  • 1x Solid steel VESA mounting plate with mounting screws
  • 1x Quick-start hardware manual & warranty documentation

Chassis Build Quality and Form Factor

Measuring approximately 135 mm x 132 mm x 58 mm (5.31" x 5.19" x 2.27") and weighing roughly 777g, the iX9 Max occupies virtually the same desk footprint as a compact desktop headphone amplifier, making it easy to tuck under a monitor or mount behind a display, especially with the included VESA mount.

The chassis is made from rather nice aluminium with a dark grey finish that resists smudges and fingerprints quite well. It did not feel particularly hot to the touch during my testing. The plastic bottom plate has airflow slots and a rubber ring that acts as its foot. Four removable plugs provide access to the screws. Once those are out, four magnets in the corners also hold the bottom plate in place.

I/O Layout and Connectivity

The sheer port density on a box this size is impressive, particularly the four high-speed USB-A ports across the front panel and the inclusion of a full SD 4.0 reader for creators, though having at least one front-mounted USB-C port would have made quick external SSD connections even more convenient.

Front Panel:

  • 4x USB 3.2 Gen 2 Type-A ports (10 Gbps, one supporting USB charging while the PC is shut down (S5))
  • 1x 3.5mm combo headphone/microphone audio jack
  • 1x Power button with an integrated status LED

Side Panel:

  • 1x SD 4.0 card reader slot

Rear Panel:

  • 2x USB4 Type-C ports (40 Gbps, DisplayPort 2.0 Alt Mode; VZMORE specifies 15W PD output on both ports and up to 100W PD input on one port)
  • 1x USB 3.2 Gen 2 Type-A port (10 Gbps)
  • 1x USB 2.0 Type-A port
  • 2x HDMI 2.0 display outputs (supporting up to 4 displays simultaneously alongside USB4)
  • 2x 2.5GbE RJ-45 Ethernet ports (useful for separate home-lab networks)
  • 1x 19V DC-In power port

3. Teardown and Hardware Specifications

What's Inside the iX9 Max?

Let’s get straight to the core specifications of the system:

  • CPU: Intel Core Ultra 9 185H (16 Cores / 22 Threads: 6x Performance Cores, 8x Efficient Cores, 2x Low-Power E-Cores, up to 5.1 GHz boost, 24MB Intel Smart Cache)
  • iGPU: Intel Arc Graphics (8 Xe-cores based on the Xe-LPG architecture, up to 2.35 GHz, hardware-accelerated ray tracing, XeSS, and AV1 hardware encode/decode)
  • NPU: Intel AI Boost delivering up to 11 TOPS of dedicated low-power AI acceleration (up to 35 TOPS total platform AI compute across CPU, GPU, and NPU)
  • RAM: 24GB onboard dual-channel LPDDR5
  • Storage: 1TB Lexar NQ7A1 2280 with an additional 2242 slot.
  • Networking: Dual 2.5GbE RJ-45 LAN, Wi-Fi 7 (EB600 wireless module), and Bluetooth 5.4
  • Card Reader: Side-mounted SD 4.0 Card Slot
  • Power Supply: 120W (19V / 6.32A) DC Barrel Jack adapter

Internal Access and Upgradability

Opening up the iX9 Max is straightforward:

  • Four Phillips-head screws sit recessed beneath removable plugs in the underside.
  • Lifting the bottom cover reveals an internal metal shield above the storage and other components.
  • Beneath the shield, you have access to the primary M.2 2280 PCIe 4.0 x4 NVMe SSD and an empty secondary M.2 2242 PCIe 4.0 x4 slot. VZMORE specifies support for up to 4TB in the 2280 slot and 1TB in the 2242 slot, for 5TB total. Underneath the primary drive sits the M.2 2230 Wi-Fi 7 (EB600) wireless module. The 24GB of dual-channel LPDDR5 is soldered onboard, so storage and the wireless module are the accessible upgrade options.

4. Thermal Engineering: The V-Cooling™ Vapour Chamber

The Technology: Vapour Chamber vs. Heat Pipes

A vapour chamber spreads heat across a broad, flat surface using the evaporation and condensation of a sealed working fluid. The complete cooling assembly, fan behaviour and power limits still determine how well a mini-PC handles sustained loads.

VZMORE’s V-Cooling™ design combines a vapour chamber with a 360-degree bottom intake and a vertical airflow path. The company claims a 40% larger heat-spreading area and 50% higher heat-transfer efficiency; those are manufacturer figures rather than measurements from this review. Heat is exhausted through the rear fin array.

Sustained Stress Testing & Thermal Results

I monitored thermal behaviour during a 60-minute session of separate Cinebench and 3DMark Steel Nomad runs at an ambient room temperature of 25°C.

  • Sustained Package Power: Reported CPU package power held around 65W during my test, with no thermal throttling observed. Package power is separate from the PC’s total draw at the wall.
  • Peak CPU Temperature: Reached 85°C during the initial turbo boost.
  • iGPU Temperature: Reached a maximum of 70°C during this test.
  • Acoustic Profile: I could not hear the system from my normal seating position during light tasks such as writing this review and watching videos. Under the sustained load, the fan produced a smooth, low-pitched airflow sound without an obvious high-frequency whine. These are subjective listening impressions rather than measured noise levels.

5. Synthetic Benchmarks

  • Geekbench 6: The iX9 Max scored 2,529 single-core and 13,519 multi-core in a valid Geekbench 6.7.1 for Windows AVX2 run. The result records the Windows power plan as Balanced; that is separate from the BIOS power limit. Validated run: https://browser.geekbench.com/v6/cpu/19388320
  • Cinebench 2024: The single-core score was 109 points, and the multi-core score was 869 points.
  • 3DMark Steel Nomad Light: Scored 3,175 points at 23.52 FPS.
  • Storage Performance: CrystalDiskMark sequential reads reached approximately 6,046 MB/s and writes reached approximately 5,002 MB/s on the pre-installed 1TB PCIe 4.0 NVMe SSD.
  • Bluetooth & Networking: I used the Ethernet ports throughout testing and connected my DAC over Bluetooth for music. Network transfers worked reliably in my use, and I experienced no Bluetooth audio dropouts. Wi-Fi performance was not tested.
  • Shared GPU Memory / VRAM: The 8 Xe-core Intel Arc iGPU shares system RAM with the CPU. I also used multiple displays without issues. Available memory for GPU workloads depends on the driver, application and other demands on system RAM.

6. Gaming Benchmarks (8 Xe-core Intel Arc iGPU)

Disclaimer: VZMORE positions the iX9 Max primarily as a productivity machine for tasks such as video/photo editing, development and data analytics. I also wanted to see what it could do with games and local AI models.

6.1 Forza Horizon 6

  • Settings: Low / Medium presets, ray tracing off, FSR set to Auto.
  • Metrics: At a 1080p output resolution with FSR set to Auto, the average was 33 FPS on Low and 29 FPS on Medium.
  • Experience: This was the game the mini-PC struggled with most. Despite averaging around 30 FPS, it felt noticeably stuttery. I would be more inclined to use it for older or less demanding titles.

6.2 Forza Horizon 5

  • Settings: Low / High presets; the 1080p High run used the medium ray-tracing setting.
  • Metrics: Reached 45 FPS at 1080p High with RT set to Medium, and 42 FPS at 1440p Low.
  • Experience: This felt significantly better on the iX9 Max than Horizon 6, and I found it more playable at these settings.

6.3 Tom Clancy’s Rainbow Six Siege (DirectX 12)

  • Settings: Low Preset.
  • Metrics: Reached an average of 58 FPS at 1440p Low.
  • Experience: This was playable in my testing, but an average of 58 FPS leaves limited headroom for players chasing consistently high frame rates in a competitive shooter.

6.4 Shadow of the Tomb Raider (DirectX 12)

  • Settings: Low / High presets, Ray Tracing Off.
  • Metrics: Reached an average of 37 FPS at 1080p High and 35 FPS at 1440p Low.
  • Experience: Fairly playable at these settings, although I would still take a console over it for this game.

7. Home Server and Linux Testing

  • OS Compatibility: I tested Ubuntu 24.04 LTS and Proxmox VE 8.2. The Intel Arc iGPU, Intel AI Boost NPU and both 2.5GbE NICs were detected. Detection is separate from successfully running an accelerated workload: GPU and NPU applications depend on the installed kernel, firmware and user space drivers. The PC also ships with Windows 11 Pro.
  • Media Server Use: Intel Quick Sync Video provides hardware-accelerated transcoding support for software such as Plex and Jellyfin. The number of simultaneous streams depends on the codecs, resolution, bitrate and HDR tone-mapping settings.
  • Virtualisation & Containers: Under Proxmox, I pinned lightweight container workloads to E-cores and reserved the 6 P-cores for heavier VMs. This arrangement worked well during my testing. The fixed 24GB of RAM is worth keeping in mind when planning how many VMs and containers to run.

8. Local AI Performance (Intel Arc Graphics, Vulkan)

I also tested local LLMs on the integrated 8 Xe-core Intel Arc GPU under Ubuntu Linux with a 7.0 generic kernel, using llama.cpp build 11900 with the Vulkan/Mesa backend and Flash Attention enabled. These GPU-based tests do not measure the Intel AI Boost NPU.

For these tests, I worked with an approximate GPU memory budget of 15.2 GiB from the system’s shared pool.

I thought this was a useful experiment, especially as on-device LLMs are becoming more common among enthusiasts. The tests were automated, and I also fed the recorded results to one of the tested models to summarise the performance. That summarisation experiment is separate from judging the models’ answer quality. This is my first time including this kind of testing, so feedback is appreciated.

Key Architectural Takeaways & Analysis

  • MoE Results: gpt-oss 20B was an interesting result on this 24GB system. Its listed model size is larger than Qwen3.5 9B (11.3 GiB versus 5.3 GiB), yet it reached 10.1 t/s in the synthetic generation test and 7.7 t/s in conversational tests, with 283.1 t/s at pp2048. Those results apply to the tested models and quantizations; this comparison does not isolate MoE architecture as the only cause of the difference.
  • Qwen vs. Gemma in These Tests: Qwen3.5 9B recorded a 21.2-second long-prompt wait, compared with 48.8 seconds for Gemma 4 E4B. That is useful evidence about these configurations, but different tokenizers, model architectures and runtime behaviour mean it is not a general ranking of how all Qwen and Gemma models scale on Arc.
  • Memory Budget & CPU Offloading: Qwen3.6 35B A3B at the tested 20.6 GiB quantization required a mixed CPU/GPU configuration and reached 3.8 t/s. The --n-cpu-moe 13 option keeps the MoE weights of the first 13 layers on the CPU, rather than offloading 13 individual experts. The test shows the performance of that configuration; it does not isolate the cause of the slowdown.

Model fit depends on the actual quantization, context, KV cache and runtime allocations, so there is no universal 10B dense / 20B MoE cut-off. Smaller quantizations can reduce memory requirements, with a potential quality trade-off.

9. Conclusion and Verdict

Pros

  • Strong 16-core / 22-thread CPU performance, with useful flexibility for multitasking and virtualisation.
  • Vapour chamber cooling handled the sustained load in my test; the system was inaudible from my normal seating position during light desktop use.
  • 8 Xe-core Intel Arc integrated graphics with hardware AV1 encode/decode, Intel Quick Sync Video and XeSS upscaling support.
  • Dual 2.5GbE networking, Wi-Fi 7, SD 4.0 card reader, and dual 40Gbps USB4 ports provide useful connectivity for creators and home labs.
  • Two PCIe 4.0 x4 M.2 slots (2280 + 2242) provide storage expansion, although I would have preferred a second 2280 slot.
  • The USB4 ports have enough bandwidth to support eGPUs if you want to make the gaming and AI experience better.

Cons

  • The soldered 24GB LPDDR5 cannot be upgraded, limiting headroom for larger VMs and local AI models, including some quantized models.
  • All four front USB ports are Type-A; a front-mounted USB-C port would improve convenience for modern flash drives and portable SSDs.
  • Rear HDMI ports are HDMI 2.0 rather than HDMI 2.1 (though DisplayPort 2.0 is available over the dual USB4 ports).
  • The 185H isn't an unlocked CPU, meaning there is an inability to overclock or undervolt it further.
  • As a relatively new brand, VZMORE has a shorter public firmware and long-term support track record to judge.

Final Verdict

For me, the iX9 Max makes most sense as a compact everyday PC with useful connectivity for a home lab. It performed well in the CPU tests, stayed unobtrusive during light use and offers plenty of ports. Gaming is more comfortable in the older titles I tested, while local AI is a useful extra if the model fits the available memory. The biggest long-term limitation is the fixed 24GB of RAM, so I would choose the system around the workloads I expect to run rather than assume I can upgrade it later.

At the $949 US MSRP for the reviewed 24GB/1TB configuration, I would be buying it for the compact design, low noise in everyday use and extensive I/O. The fixed 24GB of RAM is a significant compromise at that price, particularly for a home lab or local AI. I would recommend it most readily to someone whose workloads fit that memory ceiling and who will make good use of its ports; gaming would be a secondary use.
